    How to read this

    • A sample. A block of 150 to 300 best-of-three sets on the public ladder, played by one fixed version of the bot. The account rating carries over from sample to sample, so each sample is measured on its own.
    • Why not just the rating? The ladder rating moves with every set, depends on who happens to be online, and drifts: the same bot scored about 80 Elo apart on two different blocks of 150 sets. It is a fine scoreboard, but a poor way to compare two versions.
    • Performance Elo. The rating at which the bot’s results against the opponents it actually met, each at their own rating at the time, would be even on average. It is a maximum-likelihood fit with a 95% interval from resampling opponents, so it can be compared across samples. Early forfeits and disconnections are left out. With 150 to 300 sets the interval is still about ±70 to ±100 Elo.
    • Interleaved A/B. From s07 every set flips a coin to play the control or the variant. Both versions meet the same crowd at the same hours, so quiet nights and strong opponents hit both equally. That is much fairer than running one version after the other.
    • Rating-adjusted effect. A logistic regression of “won the set” on which version played and the opponent’s rating, converted to Elo points. Positive means the variant is better. The interval comes from resampling sets. When it contains zero, the sample cannot tell the two versions apart.
    • What it shows so far. Offline, against other bots, each variant looked 3 to 6 points better than the control. On the ladder, in both finished A/B samples the variant was not clearly better, and its point estimate was below zero. The intervals are wide, so small gains are not ruled out, but there is no sign that offline gains carry over to real players. Bot-vs-bot tests rank teams better than they rank bots. The current sample is still running; its numbers are interim.
