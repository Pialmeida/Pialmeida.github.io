Project · Self-play RL for competitive Pokémon

A bot that explains its moves

A post-game analyzer for Pokémon VGC best-of-three sets. For every turn it shows what my self-play bot would have played for each side, the next two alternatives, and an estimated win chance for each, next to what was actually played. Finished games only: the analyzer never plays live.

Why this game is hard for an AI

Chess and Go are deep, but both players see the whole board and take turns. Poker is the classic test of hidden information, but its players also act in turn. VGC (here Pokémon Champions VGC 2026, Regulation M-C) combines hidden information with simultaneous moves, chance and a three-game match.

  • Moves at the same time

    2v2

    Two Pokémon per side on the field. Each turn both players choose a move and a target for each, a switch or a Mega Evolution, at the same moment and blind. The best play is often a mix, not one fixed answer.

  • Stats you can't see

    66

    Open team sheets show species, moves, items, abilities and natures, but not stat points (0 to 32 per stat, 66 in all), which set damage and speed. Those must be read from the game.

  • A blind team preview

    90

    Before each game, both players bring 4 of their 6 and choose 2 to lead, without seeing the other's pick: 90 ways to start, per side.

  • Chance on every hit

    16

    Damage lands on one of 16 rolls; critical hits, accuracy and secondary effects add more. A good plan is a good bet, rarely a sure thing.

  • Three games, one opponent

    Bo3

    What a team showed in game one is known in game two, and both sides can change their four. Adapting, and hiding what you can, matters.

  • A huge space

    10139

    Possible team configurations alone: about 10139 (VGC-Bench, 2025). The game can't be searched whole; a player has to generalize.

How the bot is built

  • It starts from people: behaviour cloning on top players' replays.
  • It improves by self-play with R-NaD, the algorithm behind DeepNash (DeepMind's Stratego agent), which aims for play that is hard to exploit rather than one fixed best move.
  • It looks a little ahead: at play time a small search weighs its likeliest joint moves, both Pokémon together, against the opponent's replies.
  • It proves endgames: an exact solver claims a win only when it has proved it against any reply.
  • It is taught rules, not strategy: its inputs include exact damage calculations and sound bounds on the opponent's stat points, never a hint of which move is good.

How the numbers are made

  • The bot. A transformer policy trained with R-NaD (DeepNash's algorithm) by self-play, starting from behaviour cloning on top players' replays. At each turn a small search ranks its candidate joint moves, both Pokémon together, against the opponent's likely replies.
  • Win estimates are calibrated. The raw search values were too extreme, so they were mapped to observed outcomes with an isotonic fit on 1,200 bot-vs-bot sets (about 16,000 decisions), then checked on held-out games: a shown percentage is off by 1.4 points on average, and by under 4 points in every tenth of the range. Options the bot did not choose, played on purpose, are calibrated too.
  • Small gaps are noise. Options within a few points of the best are marked "≈ best", using a band (6 to 8 points) measured by searching the same positions twice.
  • A human move outside the bot's options is valued by a second search with that move added, so the opponent's reply accounts for it.
  • Proofs and estimates are kept apart. The "proven" badge comes from an exact endgame solver; everything else is labelled as an estimate.
  • Each side's numbers use only that side's view of the game, so the two needn't add up to 100%.
